# What Goes Wrong: Ranked Clause Types That Burn People

This is a research pass, not a measured meta-analysis. Regulators (FTC, CFPB, state AGs) and advocacy groups don't publish a single unified "which clause type causes the most harm" ranking — the evidence is scattered across enforcement actions, complaint-database studies, and worker/freelancer surveys that each cover one clause type. The ranking below combines (a) how large and well-documented the harmed population is, (b) how directly a regulator or major survey ties the clause to concrete financial/legal harm, and (c) how well it maps to Redline's target documents (leases, freelance agreements, ToS/subscriptions, employment-adjacent contracts). Positions 1–4 are reasonably well evidenced by hard numbers; positions 5–8 are directionally right but rest on thinner or more anecdotal evidence — flagged per item.

## Ranked list

### 1. Auto-renewal / evergreen / hard-to-cancel subscription clauses
**What it is:** The contract silently renews (and keeps charging you) unless you cancel through a process the seller controls, often deliberately friction-heavy.
**Harm:** Recurring unwanted charges, money lost to memberships people believed they'd cancelled, and cancellation flows that cost more effort than sign-up.
**Evidence:** The FTC's negative-option rulemaking rests on "dozens of enforcement actions, tens of thousands of consumer complaints" (Holland & Knight summary of FTC record). Active 2025–2026 FTC enforcement against Uber (UberOne), Amazon, Chegg, and LA Fitness targets exactly this pattern. At the state level, NY AG secured $600,000 plus consumer refunds from Equinox specifically over "not clear" auto-renewal terms and a "complex, difficult, and time-consuming" cancellation process, and separately sued Aneva Gym and other clubs for the same reason. NY legislature passed a dedicated auto-renewal law in response to complaint volume.
Sources: https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/09/ftc-steps-up-subscription-enforcement-after-click-to-cancel-rule ; https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2025/attorney-general-james-secures-600000-fitness-company-equinox-its-hard-cancel ; https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2025/attorney-general-james-sues-queens-gym-misleading-customers ; https://assembly.ny.gov/mem/Jeffrey-Dinowitz/story/93457
**Confidence in rank:** High. This is the clause type with the clearest, most active regulator paper trail (FTC national rule + repeated state AG suits), and it's the single most common real-world trigger for Redline's likely first users (ToS, subscriptions, gym/service contracts).

### 2. Arbitration clauses + class-action waivers
**What it is:** You give up the right to sue in court or join a class action; disputes go to private arbitration instead, on terms the company usually wrote.
**Harm:** Individually, arbitration clauses block access to courts and make it uneconomical to pursue small claims; in aggregate they immunize companies from class liability for widespread low-dollar harms (junk fees, mis-billing, data misuse).
**Evidence:** CFPB's 2015 arbitration study found tens of millions of consumers covered by arbitration clauses (as many as 80 million in credit cards alone), and found consumers filed only ~600 arbitration cases/year on average versus companies' broad coverage. Prevalence of class-action waivers specifically grew from 16% of company contracts in 2012 to 43% in 2014 per cited industry survey data.
Sources: https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201503_cfpb_factsheet_arbitration-study.pdf ; https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-study-finds-that-arbitration-agreements-limit-relief-for-consumers/ ; https://centerjd.org/content/fact-sheet-forced-arbitration-clauses-and-class-actions-waivers-numbers ; https://www.epi.org/publication/the-arbitration-epidemic/
**Confidence in rank:** High on prevalence and regulatory documentation; medium on "burns people" in the acute, immediate sense Redline cares about — arbitration harm is usually realized only if something else goes wrong first (it's a harm-multiplier clause, not a standalone trigger). Worth flagging prominently but users may not "feel" it the way they feel an auto-renewal charge.

### 3. Payment terms / non-payment and late payment (freelance & small-business contracts)
**What it is:** Vague or one-sided payment terms — no clear due date, no late-payment penalty for the client, net-60/net-90 terms, or no recourse if the client simply doesn't pay.
**Harm:** Direct, large income loss for independent workers; this is arguably the single most financially damaging category for the freelance-agreement segment of Redline's scope, even though it's not a single boilerplate "clause type" so much as an absence-of-protection pattern.
**Evidence:** Freelancers Union 2015 report: half of freelancers had payment trouble in a given year, 71% had trouble at some point in their career, average loss $5,968/year (13% of income). A 2022 NY-focused Freelancers Union survey found 60–62% of NY freelancers report never being paid for some work performed, 91% report late/overdue payment at some point, 53% report losing as much as $10,000 total to nonpayment.
Sources: https://www.onlabor.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/FU_NonpaymentReport_r3.pdf ; https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2022/05/12/over-60-of-ny-freelancers-report-not-being-paid-for-work-performed/ ; https://nwu.org/survey-shows-that-62-of-ny-freelancers-experience-non-payment-need-freelance-isnt-free-law-statewide/
**Confidence in rank:** High on harm magnitude for freelancers specifically; note this is somewhat different in kind from the other entries (it's often what a contract *fails to specify* rather than a clause that traps you), so Redline's "risky clause" framing should include "missing/weak payment-terms protection" as a flaggable pattern, not only adversarial boilerplate.

### 4. Non-compete / non-solicit clauses
**What it is:** A clause barring you from working for a competitor or soliciting former clients/colleagues after the relationship ends, often broad in scope, duration, or geography.
**Harm:** Suppressed wages, blocked job mobility, and — per FTC's own rulemaking record — disproportionate impact on low-wage workers who often don't understand what they signed.
**Evidence:** An estimated 1 in 5 US workers (~30 million) are bound by a noncompete. FTC's enforcement case against Rollins (pest control) covered 18,000+ employees "regardless of role," mostly relatively low-wage earners — cited by FTC as an example of overbroad application. FTC's proposed nationwide ban was withdrawn in Sept 2025, but the agency shifted to targeted enforcement and flagged this as a 2026–2030 strategic priority.
Sources: https://www.npr.org/2023/01/13/1148446019/ftc-rule-ban-noncompetes-low-wage-workers-trade-secrets ; https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2026-may/ftc-actions-worker-noncompetes/ ; https://www.epi.org/publication/epi-comment-on-the-ftcs-non-compete-clause-rule/
**Confidence in rank:** Medium-high. Strong regulator attention and a large affected population, but weaker on "acute financial burn" evidence compared to items 1–3 — harm is diffuse (lower lifetime wages) rather than a single dollar-figure event, and it's less central to Redline's stated document set (freelance/ToS/lease) unless employment agreements are in scope.

### 5. Joint-and-several liability (leases with roommates/co-tenants)
**What it is:** Each named tenant is fully liable for the entire lease obligation (full rent, full damages) — not just their proportional share — if a co-tenant doesn't pay or damages the unit.
**Harm:** A tenant can be on the hook for a roommate's unpaid rent or damage, sometimes leading to eviction or collections for debt they didn't personally incur.
**Evidence:** National Consumer Law Center's 2023 comments on rental-housing junk fees document this and related unfair lease terms as a live concern in tenant advocacy work; general tenant-resource explainers (Tenant Resource Center, RentPost) corroborate the mechanism and its dispute pattern.
Sources: https://www.nclc.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Final-NCLC-et-al.-Group-Comments-re-Rental-Housing-Junk-Fees-with-Addenda.pdf ; https://www.tenantresourcecenter.org/joint_and_several_liability
**Confidence in rank:** Lower/medium. I could not find a regulator complaint-volume figure specific to joint-and-several liability (as distinct from lease fees generally); the NCLC source is substantive but the ranking position here is more impressionistic, based on how frequently this pattern appears in tenant-advocacy literature rather than a hard complaint count.

### 6. IP assignment clauses (freelance / contractor agreements)
**What it is:** A clause transferring ownership of everything a freelancer creates — sometimes including pre-existing tools, frameworks, or side work — to the client, often worded far more broadly than the actual deliverable.
**Harm:** Freelancers can lose ownership of reusable tools/IP built outside the specific engagement; disputes are expensive to resolve even when the freelancer is in the right.
**Evidence:** Industry practitioner sources describe scope/IP-ownership disputes as a leading source of contractor conflict, with one cited real-world case costing $15,000 in legal fees over a disputed testing-framework ownership claim after weak IP carve-out language.
Sources: https://www.usepact.org/blog/post/freelancer-ip-rights-guide ; https://clauseshield.app/blog/ip-ownership-clauses-freelancers
**Confidence in rank:** Low-medium. No regulator or large-sample survey data found — evidence here is practitioner/anecdotal (law-firm and product blogs), not a complaint database or enforcement record. Include it based on direct relevance to Redline's freelance-agreement use case, but flag the ranking as impressionistic.

### 7. Unilateral amendment ("we may change these terms at any time") clauses
**What it is:** The company reserves the right to change the contract's material terms unilaterally, often with only a notice posted on a website rather than direct notice to the user.
**Harm:** Users can be bound by terms they never agreed to and may not know changed (fee increases, new data uses, new arbitration terms), with limited practical ability to object because switching costs are high.
**Evidence:** FTC's own Tech@FTC policy blog states quietly changing ToS "could be unfair or deceptive" and signals enforcement interest. CFPB's proposed contract-terms rule would have banned unilateral-amendment clauses outright in consumer financial contracts. Commentary source notes litigation over these changes is "relatively rare" even though consumer complaints are common.
Sources: https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2024/02/ai-other-companies-quietly-changing-your-terms-service-could-be-unfair-or-deceptive ; https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2025/01/16/will-the-cfpbs-proposed-rule-on-contract-terms-and-conditions-affect-arbitration-clauses/ ; https://ccbjournal.com/articles/unilaterally-amended-terms-of-service-agreements-put-customers-in-a-bind
**Confidence in rank:** Low-medium. Real regulatory attention (FTC + proposed CFPB rule) but explicitly noted as low-litigation / complaint-driven-but-diffuse harm — it's the clause that enables other harms (fee hikes, new arbitration terms) more than a standalone injury, similar to arbitration clauses in that respect but with even less standalone complaint data.

### 8. Indemnification and limitation-of-liability clauses (commercial/service contracts)
**What it is:** Indemnification shifts responsibility for third-party claims/legal costs to one party; limitation-of-liability caps how much a party can ever be forced to pay, regardless of actual damages.
**Harm:** Small businesses/freelancers can end up liable for the client's legal costs or business losses via broad indemnification language, or find their own recovery capped far below actual harm if the counterparty caused damage.
**Evidence:** A small-business-focused law firm identifies indemnity (along with non-compete and termination) provisions as leading causes of litigation for small businesses due to imprecise drafting; general commercial-law sources confirm these are near-universal clauses whose vague wording ("any and all claims") drives disputes.
Sources: https://www.aikenslawfirm.com/the-contract-clauses-that-create-the-most-litigation-for-small-businesses/ ; https://www.gleamlaw.com/blog/business-litigation/indemnification-litigation/
**Confidence in rank:** Low. No complaint-database or survey quantification found — this entry rests on law-firm blog commentary, not measured data. Included because it recurs constantly in freelance/vendor contracts (Redline's core use case) and practitioners consistently flag it, but the ranking position is the least evidence-backed on this list.

## What I could not find
- No single regulator or industry survey ranks clause types head-to-head by complaint volume; the ranking above is a synthesis across separate, differently-scoped evidence bases (FTC negative-option record, CFPB arbitration study, Freelancers Union surveys, NCLC lease comments, and law-firm commercial-litigation commentary), not a single measured ranking.
- Could not find CFPB complaint-database numbers broken out specifically by "junk fees" as a category (only general arbitration-clause coverage stats).
- Could not find hard complaint-volume or dollar-loss data for: joint-and-several lease liability specifically (as opposed to lease fees generally), IP-assignment disputes in freelance contracts, unilateral-amendment clauses, or indemnification/limitation-of-liability clauses — these four rest on advocacy-group or practitioner commentary rather than quantified complaint/enforcement data, and are explicitly flagged as lower-confidence above.
- Did not find data on personal guarantees, kill fees, or fee escalators specifically (candidates mentioned in the brief); ran out of search budget before covering them and would not want to speculate on their ranking without evidence.
