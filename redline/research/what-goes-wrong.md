# What Goes Wrong: Clause Types That Actually Burn People

Research for Redline (contract/lease/freelance/ToS risk-flagging tool). Ranked by strength and volume of evidence that the clause type causes real-world harm — enforcement actions, complaint volume, or survey data — not by theoretical risk.

---

## 1. Auto-renewal / negative-option / "evergreen" subscription terms
**What it does:** Contract silently renews (and re-bills) unless the customer affirmatively cancels within a window; cancellation is often made deliberately harder than signup.
**Harm mechanism:** Customer is billed for a service they didn't mean to keep, discovers it months later, and faces a friction-heavy cancellation process.
**Appears in:** ToS / consumer subscriptions (SaaS, media, gyms, apps) — the paradigm case; also creeps into B2B SaaS auto-renewal terms.
**Evidence:** The FTC has an active, sustained enforcement wave against subscription/auto-renewal practices — named actions against Match.com, Chegg, and Amazon for opaque disclosures and hard-to-cancel flows. The FTC's "Click to Cancel" rule was vacated by the 8th Circuit in 2025 on procedural grounds but the FTC is pursuing an ANPRM (submitted to OMB Jan 2026) and continues bringing cases under ROSCA/FTC Act regardless of the rule's status.
**Strength:** Hard — real, ongoing federal enforcement actions naming specific companies, plus formal rulemaking activity.
**Source:** https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/09/ftc-steps-up-subscription-enforcement-after-click-to-cancel-rule ; https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/05/ftc-revives-clicktocancel-rule-new-risks-for-subscription-businesses

## 2. Forced arbitration + class-action waiver
**What it does:** Requires disputes to go to private, individual arbitration instead of court; bars joining or bringing a class action.
**Harm mechanism:** Individually, disputed amounts are usually too small to justify arbitration costs, so the clause functions to suppress nearly all consumer recourse, not just redirect it.
**Appears in:** ToS (financial services, consumer apps) most heavily studied; also common in freelance platform ToS and employment agreements.
<br>**Evidence:** CFPB's formal arbitration study found >75% of consumers didn't know whether they were bound by an arbitration clause in financial contracts, and fewer than 7% of those who were realized it blocked them from suing. In a 2010–2012 sample across six markets, only 1,847 arbitration disputes were filed (>20% of those *by the company*, not the consumer) versus 3,462 individual federal lawsuits — evidence the clause suppresses claims rather than redirecting them. A separate analysis of CFPB complaint data found 2,393 of 5.6M complaints specifically referenced arbitration-related obstacles.
**Strength:** Hard — this is a federal regulatory study, not an advocacy estimate, though scope is financial-services contracts specifically (extrapolation to general ToS is reasonable but not directly measured).
**Source:** https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201503_cfpb_factsheet_arbitration-study.pdf ; https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-study-finds-that-arbitration-agreements-limit-relief-for-consumers/

## 3. Late/non-payment exposure in freelance contracts (weak kill-fee / payment-terms protection)
**What it does:** Contract lacks (or client ignores) enforceable payment deadlines, late fees, or kill fees, leaving the freelancer with no leverage when a client delays or refuses payment.
**Harm mechanism:** Freelancer delivers work and has no contractual teeth to force payment; recourse is informal chasing (email/calls) with low success.
**Appears in:** Freelance / independent-contractor agreements almost exclusively.
**Evidence:** Freelancers Union survey: 71% of freelancers report struggling to collect payment for work at least once in their career; 91% report late/overdue payment at least once; 53% report losing up to $10,000 to nonpayment over their career; 79% who chased unpaid invoices did so via their own demands (email/calls) rather than legal action, with most needing 3+ follow-ups. A separate 2022 NY-specific survey found 60–62% of NY freelancers report not being paid for completed work.
**Strength:** Hard survey data (large N, advocacy-org-run but methodologically a real survey), though it's self-reported and Freelancers Union has an advocacy interest in the topic.
**Source:** https://www.onlabor.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/FU_NonpaymentReport_r3.pdf ; https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2022/05/12/over-60-of-ny-freelancers-report-not-being-paid-for-work-performed/

## 4. Security deposit / move-out damage clauses (leases)
**What it does:** Governs what landlords can deduct from a deposit at move-out for "damage" versus normal wear and tear, and the timeline/process for returning it.
**Harm mechanism:** Ambiguous or landlord-favorable damage/deduction language + weak return deadlines lets landlords withhold deposits with little friction; tenant has to fight to get money back.
**Appears in:** Residential leases — the dominant tenant-side complaint category.
**Evidence:** Wisconsin's Bureau of Consumer Protection reports landlord-tenant issues as the #1 consumer complaint category for the fourth consecutive year (2025), with security deposit disputes central to that category. Rental Protection Agency data ranks security deposit disputes #1 on its list of apartment complaints. Nearly 5,000 NY renters filed complaints for missing security deposits in a single year per a Gothamist-reported 2024 dataset.
**Strength:** Moderate-hard — state consumer-protection agency ranking is solid, but I could not find a single national hard dataset quantifying deposit-dispute volume; evidence is a mix of state-level counts and secondary reporting.
**Source:** https://www.wizmnews.com/2026/02/05/landlord-tenant-disputes-top-consumer-complaint-in-2025/ ; https://american-apartment-owners-association.org/property-management/latest-news/10-most-common-apartment-complaints/

## 5. Non-compete clauses
**What it does:** Bars a worker from taking a job with a competitor, or starting a competing business, for a period after leaving.
**Harm mechanism:** Suppresses wages and mobility even for workers never actually sued over it, by chilling job search and negotiating leverage.
**Appears in:** Employment agreements primarily; also independent-contractor and freelance agreements, and sometimes B2B channel/reseller agreements.
**Evidence:** FTC's 2024 rulemaking record: ~1 in 5 US workers (~30 million) is subject to a non-compete; FTC's own economic estimate is the ban would raise US workers' earnings $250–296B/year; research cited in the rulemaking found nationwide ban would close racial/gender wage gaps 3.6–9.1%. Rule itself is currently enjoined by a federal district court (Aug 2024) and not in effect, but the underlying prevalence/harm data comes from the FTC's rulemaking record, not just advocacy.
**Strength:** Hard for prevalence (30M workers) and moderately hard for wage-harm estimates (FTC's own modeled figures, contested by industry).
**Source:** https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-announces-rule-banning-noncompetes ; https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/noncompete_nprm_fact_sheet.pdf

## 6. Unilateral fee changes / "junk fees" (late fees, processing fees, add-on rental fees)
**What it does:** Contract permits the counterparty to add or increase fees — late fees, "convenience" or payment-processing fees, escalators — often without renegotiation.
**Harm mechanism:** Costs balloon after signing in ways the consumer didn't clearly agree to or can't avoid.
**Appears in:** Leases (rent payment processing fees), consumer financial contracts (credit cards), ToS (subscription/BNPL products).
**Evidence:** CFPB report: credit card issuers charged $12B in late fees in 2020; CFPB's now-contested rule sought to cut the standard late fee cap from $41 to $8. CFPB has received 1,700+ complaints specifically about rental-debt-collection and rental "junk fees," including mandatory rent-payment-processing fees.
**Strength:** Hard — CFPB's own aggregated industry data for credit-card late fees; the rental-fee complaint count (1,700+) is a real complaint-volume figure though smaller in scale.
**Source:** https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-uncovers-illegal-junk-fees-on-bank-accounts-mortgages-and-student-and-auto-loans/ ; https://rentalhousingjournal.com/consumer-bureau-targets-rental-debt-collection-junk-fees/

## 7. Unilateral amendment ("we may change these terms at any time")
**What it does:** Lets the drafting party change the contract terms (price, arbitration terms, data use, etc.) after the fact, typically with only passive notice (a posted update, not direct notice).
**Harm mechanism:** User is bound by terms they never affirmatively agreed to; because litigation is rare relative to how often users notice and are annoyed by it, the clause mostly functions as a standing risk rather than a frequently-litigated one.
**Appears in:** ToS almost universally (Facebook, Twitter/X, Instagram, Google-style clauses are described as "ubiquitous"); increasingly in SaaS subscription agreements.
**Evidence:** Legal commentary confirms these clauses are near-universal across major platforms, but litigation is comparatively rare — a Ninth Circuit ruling (*Douglas v. Talk America*, 2007) held a company cannot unilaterally amend a ToS and bind existing users without actual notice, and a more recent N.D. Ohio decision similarly limited enforceability. The FTC has separately warned that quietly changing data-use terms via a retroactive ToS amendment can be an unfair/deceptive practice.
**Strength:** Moderate — legal/precedent evidence is solid, but this is a case-law and regulatory-warning pattern rather than a complaint-volume or enforcement-count metric; direct "how often does this burn people" data was not found.
**Source:** https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=e79c3c06-ec1e-4040-934f-b0fb21d54eb0 ; https://ccbjournal.com/articles/unilaterally-amended-terms-of-service-agreements-put-customers-in-a-bind

## 8. Liability caps and indemnification scope (B2B / SaaS)
**What it does:** Caps how much the vendor owes if something goes wrong (often capped at fees paid), and defines who bears legal costs and damages from third-party claims (IP infringement, data breach).
**Harm mechanism:** When something does go wrong (outage, breach, IP claim), the customer discovers the cap and indemnification carve-outs limit recovery far below actual damages — the risk is invisible until the incident happens.
**Appears in:** B2B SaaS and software licensing agreements almost exclusively; less relevant to consumer ToS or residential leases.
**Evidence:** Limitation-of-liability is reported as the #1 most negotiated contract clause globally for over a decade per the World Commerce & Contracting (WorldCC) annual survey, and limitation-of-liability plus termination rights are described as the two clauses most frequently disputed when a SaaS relationship breaks down. Market-standard caps run around 12 months of fees, with indemnification often carved out via a separate "super-cap."
**Strength:** Moderate — the WorldCC "most negotiated clause" statistic is a real, oft-cited industry survey, but it measures negotiation frequency, not documented harm/dispute outcomes; I could not find a hard count of disputes or enforcement actions tied to this specific clause.
**Source:** https://gc.ai/blog/saas-agreement ; https://www.hyperstart.com/blog/limitation-of-liability/

## Lower-confidence / anecdotal-only findings (included for completeness, not ranked)

- **IP assignment / work-for-hire ambiguity in freelance contracts:** Widely discussed in freelancer-legal-guide content as a common source of disputes over deliverable ownership, but I could not find a quantified complaint or dispute dataset — only qualitative practitioner commentary. Source: https://patentpc.com/blog/how-to-prevent-ip-ownership-disputes-with-contractors-and-freelancers
- **Personal guarantees in commercial leases:** Consistently described by commercial real estate/legal sources as the single biggest source of catastrophic personal financial exposure for small-business tenants (unlimited personal liability for the full lease term), but no default-rate or dispute-volume statistics were found in this search. Source: https://theleasinglawyers.com/post/personal-guarantee-commercial-lease

## What I could not find

- No unified, cross-document-type dataset ranking clause types by complaint volume (each source covers one document type — leases, ToS, freelance, or B2B — not a common taxonomy).
- No hard statistics on non-solicit clauses specifically (distinct from non-competes) or on exclusivity clauses in freelance/vendor agreements.
- No dispute or default-rate data for personal guarantees in commercial leases.
- No quantified dataset for IP-assignment disputes in freelance work — only qualitative legal-guide commentary.
- Arbitration-clause hard data (CFPB study) is specific to financial-services contracts; I did not find an equivalent rigorous study for arbitration clauses in general consumer ToS or freelance-platform terms, though qualitative sources describe the clauses as similarly near-universal there.
- Ranking above 5–6 is somewhat impressionistic: items are ordered by strength/directness of evidence for "this clause type causes measurable harm," not by a single common metric, since no source measures all clause types on the same scale.
